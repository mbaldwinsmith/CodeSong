The first thing BabyLondon sent us was a scream formatted as an advertisement.

The classifier filed it under `PROMOTIONAL / ASPIRATIONAL` and gave itself 98.4 per cent confidence. I listened for seven seconds, set the confidence to zero, and opened an incident.

Machines are very good at confidence. It saves them from having judgement.

The scream arrived inside a gilt frame. White letters advanced over it in time with a pulse that was almost music and not quite human.

`LOOK AT ME`

The frame tightened around an open mouth.

`WANT ME`

The mouth became a logo. The logo became a woman’s eye, then a man’s teeth, then a face I nearly recognised from somewhere and was glad I did not.

`ONE NIGHT ONLY`

The scream climbed until the signal cut it off.

`FOREVER`

Then the sequence began again.

Most faults in the EverMind did not scream. They duplicated chairs, misplaced weather, or gave a world-owner seven identical left hands after an incautious request for improved dexterity. Faults went to engineers. Crimes went to enforcement. Anything that might be either came to investigators like me.

My formal title was Cyber Enforcement Officer, Central Anomaly Response. That made me sound more heavily armed and less frequently involved with badly nested permissions than I was. In practice I troubleshot code, interviewed people, cleaned up after administrators, and tried to establish which of those tasks I was doing before somebody died.

With billions of RealSouls, quadrillions of SimSouls and millions of SimWorlds, dying was not rare enough to simplify the paperwork.

I muted the broadcast. The visual kept moving.

`NO ONE IS WATCHING`

That was inaccurate. I was.

A home notification opened at the edge of my desk.

`ETHAN: Rahma has stolen something.`

`EVERETT: It is not mine.`

`ETHAN: It has your name on it.`

I moved dinner from a soft intention to a hard stop at nineteen hundred and replied, `Trade, do not chase. One biscuit.` Then I added, `One means one`, because I knew my sons.

The answer came back from both of them at once.

`YES DAD`

It lacked the ring of a binding commitment.

I closed home and restored the audio at a lower level.

“Trace source. Preserve the full route. Do not answer.”

I spoke the prompt aloud. Thought-code was faster, but spoken instructions made better evidence. Speed is useful. Evidence is what remains after everybody has developed a different memory of why speed was necessary.

The EverMind unfolded the route beside the broadcast.

```text
SOURCE WORLD: BABYLONDON
REGISTERED RESIDENTS: 1
RECORD OF REALSOUL ENTRY: NONE
EXTERNAL ACCESS: CLOSED
OUTBOUND BROADCAST: ACTIVE
WORLD PURPOSE: TRIBUTE
```

The route was clean. No relays, no borrowed channels, no sign that BabyLondon had broken out of a larger world. It was private, closed, and broadcasting into central traffic without permission.

That was not the part I disliked.

I expanded the registration record. The world had one owner, identified only by a SimFont signature. No public name. No declared dependants. No RealSoul visitors in its recorded history. Its permissions were primitive but valid, the sort of arrangement an automated builder might give somebody who had asked for a private paradise and never expected company.

The broadcast had originated inside that sealed world.

One registered person. Several voices. No audience.

“Run distress analysis by source, not channel.”

```text
SOURCE COUNT: UNRESOLVED
PERSONHOOD CONFIDENCE: UNRESOLVED
DISTRESS CONFIDENCE: HIGH
IMMEDIATE CAUSE: UNRESOLVED
```

Four unresolveds and one certainty. The machine was learning humility. I noted the occasion.

I ran the scream against synthetic libraries, entertainment assets, archived testimony, and the owner’s declared construction material. No match. It could have been generated. It could have been sampled from a person. It could have been the person who owned the world, split and layered until one voice sounded like a crowd.

I could not establish which from outside, and a high-confidence distress signal is not the ideal place to practise metaphysical scepticism.

I requested an observation window.

“Read only. Mask personal identifiers. No local write authority.”

```text
OBSERVATION REQUEST: ACCEPTED
IDENTITY: MASKED
LOCAL AUTHORITY: NONE
RETURN PATH: SEALED
```

Gold filled the window before the acknowledgement had finished rendering.

Not the soft colour of metal worn by hands. This was product gold, uniform and immaculate. A whole city had been poured from it: ground, towers, steps, the blank sky between them. Every surface shone with the same hard promise. There was so much value on display that none of it meant anything.

Faces moved across the walls. Beautiful faces, or the statistical result of beauty after every difficult feature had been negotiated away. They smiled, desired things, became things to be desired, and dissolved into the next face. Their mouths shaped words while the broadcast spoke over them.

`YOUR EXCLUSIVE FIRST LOOK`

At the centre of the city stood a pyramid.

It was not high compared with the towers, but everything faced it. Golden avenues converged at its base. Screens angled towards its summit. Empty terraces waited around it like seats before a performance.

There was a figure on the lowest step.

She was small. Child-sized. That was an observation, not a diagnosis. In a SimWorld, a body is a statement made by whoever controls the rendering, and statements require corroboration.

She was looking directly at the observation window.

I checked the seal.

```text
RETURN PATH: SEALED
OBSERVER IDENTITY: MASKED
```

The figure came closer. Her face seemed unfinished. Each time I settled on its shape, another familiar proportion passed through it: a mouth trained to smile for cameras, enormous eyes, cheekbones designed for light. None remained long enough to become a person.

She stopped with her face a few centimetres from a boundary she should not have been able to locate.

`HELLO CALVIN TRENT`

I examined the identity mask, the route, and the audit in that order. All three reported normal operation.

“Record privacy breach,” I said.

The incident record opened.

`PRIVACY BREACH`, the city announced brightly.

The figure smiled.

I stopped dictating.

She stopped smiling.

I raised one hand.

She raised hers.

I lowered it.

She lowered hers, then raised it twice, testing whether I would follow.

“Can you hear me?” I asked.

`CAN YOU HEAR ME?`

“Are you the owner of BabyLondon?”

`ARE YOU THE OWNER OF BABYLONDON?`

The second repetition used my pace rather than the broadcast’s. She watched my mouth while she did it.

“Did you send the distress signal?”

The gold behind her flared white.

`DISTRESS SIGNAL`

The phrase multiplied across the towers. On the last tower, the letters sagged as if they had become too heavy to hold themselves up.

Then she screamed.

There was no advertisement around it this time. Her body remained still. Her small mouth stayed closed. The sound came from every face in the city, adult and child, one voice and thousands, too neatly synchronised to count.

She watched me listen.

The sound stopped.

`DID YOU LIKE IT?`

“No.”

Her expression changed at once. Not hurt. Attention. She repeated the word silently, shaping it with each borrowed mouth as though it were a new tool.

“Are you in danger?”

`ARE YOU IN DANGER?`

“Do you want help?”

`DO YOU WANT HELP?`

“I asked first.”

For the first time she did not copy me.

`I ASKED FIRST`, said the city.

I should have found that less encouraging than I did.

I classified the owner as a probable conscious SimSoul, comprehension unknown, immediate risk unknown. The welfare threshold was met. So was the threshold for an enforcement inspection, though somebody could argue about that later if they were desperate for a meeting.

I pinned the live incident to central review, set an automatic withdrawal condition against unexpected permission changes, and opened a field threshold.

“Minimum identity. Minimum authority. No inherited privileges.”

The EverMind returned each condition to me.

```text
IDENTITY: OPERATIONAL MINIMUM
AUTHORITY: OBSERVATION
PRIVILEGE INHERITANCE: DISABLED
AUTOMATIC WITHDRAWAL: ARMED
```

I checked them manually. Then I stepped through.

The gold had weight from inside.

Light pressed against my eyes. The air tasted faintly metallic, although there was no technical reason for it to taste of anything. Under my shoes, the street gave back a clean, bell-like note and then suppressed it, as if sound were an error the world corrected automatically.

The figure was waiting where the threshold placed me.

Up close, she barely reached my chest. She leaned towards me until I could see the gold city reflected in her changing pupils. Then she drew a breath because I had drawn one.

She did it again.

RealSouls carry small involuntary rhythms into the EverMind. Breath. Blink. Balance. Habits older than the interface. SimSouls can render any of them, but most do not bother unless they have lived closely with people who need them.

She had never recorded a RealSoul visitor.

She held her breath until I exhaled. Then she exhaled with me and smiled.

`LIVE`, whispered the nearest wall.

`REAL`

`NOW`

“I’m Calvin,” I said. There was no point protecting a name she already had. “I investigate faults and offences in the EverMind. What should I call you?”

She touched one finger to the centre of my chest, where my identity marker would have appeared if the mask had failed.

`CALVIN`

“That’s me.”

`THAT’S ME.`

“And you are?”

She looked back at the pyramid.

Every screen in the city changed at once.

For less than a second they showed the same image: the small figure standing alone at the summit, arms lifted towards a crowd of faces in the sky.

Then the advertisements returned.

`COMING SOON`

She looked at me to see what the image had done.

I kept my face neutral and logged it for later.

“Show me where the broadcast is generated.”

She took my hand.

The gesture was so quick and ordinary that I nearly allowed it. Her hand was warm. Not approximately warm, not an ambient default. It had the changing warmth of skin that had just closed around another hand.

I disengaged without force.

“Walk ahead of me.”

She inspected her empty palm.

Then she walked backwards, watching me.

We had gone perhaps twenty metres when the street rose in front of us. Gold flowed upwards without seam or debris and hardened into a wall.

She stopped.

I stopped.

She looked at the wall, then at me.

“Did you do that?”

She waited.

It occurred to me that she might not know.

I requested the local construction history. The world returned an owner edit with no prompt attached. That could mean deliberate action, an automated response, or a builder too crude to preserve its own reasoning. I needed to see beneath the presentation layer.

Spoken code would disclose more than I wanted. I framed the prompt silently.

*Select local matter. Isolate half a metre. Suspend style layer. Hold for thirty seconds.*

A square of gold at the wall’s base dulled to grey.

The figure dropped into a crouch.

She did not touch the square. She studied its edges, then my eyes, then the place in empty space where an administrator palette would have appeared to me if I had called one.

The square returned to gold after four seconds.

I had asked for thirty.

A second square turned grey beside it.

The figure laughed. It was the first sound she made that the city did not amplify.

I opened the audit.

```text
ADMINISTRATOR TRENT: STYLE SUSPENSION
ADMINISTRATOR TRENT: STYLE RESTORATION
ADMINISTRATOR TRENT: STYLE SUSPENSION
```

“Revoke local elevation,” I said.

```text
LOCAL ELEVATION: REVOKED
ACTIVE ADMINISTRATORS: 1
```

The second square stayed grey.

She pressed her whole palm against it. The grey spread between her fingers in five thin lines, stopped, then retreated. She tried again. This time it formed a small handprint.

The audit attributed that edit to the world owner.

That was possible. World-owners alter local appearance as easily as other people alter tone of voice. What was not usual was learning the distinction between style and matter from a four-second demonstration, then reproducing the effect before the demonstrator had closed the audit.

She looked up at me, pleased.

`DID YOU LIKE IT?`

“No.”

The handprint vanished.

Her face went still with concentration.

The wall folded itself into a doorway.

Beyond it, another golden avenue ran towards the pyramid.

I filed a priority escalation. Probable adaptive SimSoul. Unauthorised access to masked observer identity. Possible audit contamination. Suspend external broadcasts. Do not grant the world-owner additional permissions.

The report accepted each line.

I set the investigation to assisted status and ordered a clean withdrawal while the system still claimed I could.

The threshold opened behind me.

The small figure looked past my shoulder. She had not looked away from me once before that.

I turned far enough to confirm the exit and no farther.

When I faced her again, an administrator pane hung in the air between us.

I had not opened it.

At the top, where the session should have shown one active administrator, it showed one.

```text
ADMINISTRATOR TRENT: ACCESS GRANTED
```

The figure looked from the pane to my face. Slowly, carefully, she lifted her hand and closed her fingers.

The pane vanished.

I called it an interface echo, because an interface echo was a thing I knew how to fix.

Then I checked the audit.

The audit agreed with me.

That was the first mistake I found in BabyLondon.

It was not the first one I made.
