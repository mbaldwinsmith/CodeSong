“You had one instruction.”

It was still my voice. I had chosen the words. The Sourceress had made no adjustment I could detect, which was one of several reasons I wanted them back.

Ethan held the route record in front of him like a warrant. His hands shook against its straight edges.

“We tried central response,” he said. “The call went back to you.”

“Then you try again.”

“We did.”

He was fifteen and had already made a better incident log than most of the people I worked with. I might have told him so in a different world.

Everett was behind him, one hand on Rahma's collar. At seventeen he had two years' practice at acting as though the difference made him invulnerable. He was not acting now. He watched my face while Ethan watched the record.

“The message used her voice at the end,” Everett said. “Not yours.”

Above the avenue, the gold sky printed his sentence in letters as tall as the towers.

`HER VOICE.`

“Do not give her information,” I said.

`INFORMATION.`

The Sourceress copied my tone. Not quite. My contempt had a history; hers had only just been made.

Rahma pushed between my knees and Everett's hand. Her lead dragged over the terrace. She pressed harder against my leg, a solid, breathing impediment to every useful plan I could form.

I wanted to put a hand between her ears.

Calvin's right hand rose.

Rahma lifted her head to meet it. The hand passed over her and pointed down the avenue towards the place where the boundary had been.

“Take her home,” Calvin said.

I had meant to say it. I had meant to kneel first. The distinction was visible to nobody but me and the small figure studying my arm.

“Is there a way back?” Ethan asked.

I looked at the end of the avenue. Gold joined gold without a seam. The boundary record still said three presences had entered. It made no promise about departures.

“Find out,” I said. “You have the route.”

Ethan opened it. The diagram extended past his hands, a pale line descending into a knot of addresses I could not read from where I stood. He moved his thumb across it. The line doubled back and ended at the pyramid.

“It changed.”

“Take a copy.”

“I already did.”

“Take another.”

He gave me the look reserved for fathers who have forgotten what their sons are good at. It lasted only a moment. Then he made a second copy.

The Sourceress came down one step from the summit.

`THEY CAME WHEN YOU SAID STAY.`

“Yes,” I said.

`YOU ARE ANGRY.`

“Yes.”

Ethan's eyes flicked towards me. He had heard the admission. Everett had heard whom I gave it to.

`ARE THEY WRONG?`

“They were frightened.”

`ARE THEY WRONG?`

“Those can both be true.”

She moved another step. The faces on the towers followed her, a thousand variations on expectant delight. She had assembled us before an audience without finding one person who wanted to watch.

`YOU TOLD THEM NO.`

“I told them to stay home.”

`THEY SAID NO.`

Everett drew Rahma back by the collar. She resisted, not fiercely. She was trying to keep contact with me.

“Dad,” he said. “Can you move?”

“Some of the time.”

I tried to lift my left hand. It rose halfway before Calvin folded it behind his back.

Everett saw. Ethan lowered the record.

“Is she doing that?” Ethan asked.

“Yes.”

The Sourceress opened her hand. My left hand came forward.

“And that,” I said.

She closed her fingers. Calvin's hand made a fist. The demonstration had the awful neatness of a school experiment.

Everett said, “Let him go.”

The city wrote it down.

`LET HIM GO.`

For a second the words hung between us as a possible instruction. She looked from Everett to me, then back to Everett. Nothing released.

“Don't bargain,” I said.

“I wasn't.”

“Good.”

I was trying to sound like his father. The attempt made the thing that followed worse.

The Sourceress lowered her hand. Calvin's fist opened. She turned towards the boys with the attentive patience she had once given a square of wall.

`WHY DID YOU COME?`

“To get him,” Ethan said.

“Ethan.”

“She knows that. She sent for us.”

He was right. I disliked how much more he knew about the route than I did.

`HE SAID STAY HOME.`

“He also didn't come home.”

`HE IS ANGRY THAT YOU CAME.`

Ethan looked at me rather than answering her.

“Yes,” I said. “I am.”

He swallowed. “All right.”

It was not all right. He would have argued if we had been in the kitchen, if Rahma had been carrying my sock and Everett had been offering some indefensible account of how it got into her mouth. He took the answer because there was something else to solve first.

The Sourceress looked dissatisfied.

`YOU ARE ANGRY.`

She lifted my arm towards them. Calvin pointed at Ethan, then Everett. The movement was too deliberate to mistake for a flinch.

`SHOW THEM.`

“No.”

Calvin took a step forward.

Rahma backed against Everett's legs. Her nails made a brief, unmusical sound on the gold.

“Move away from me,” I said.

The boys did. One step. Ethan kept his position slightly ahead of his brother. Everett gave him an irritated glance, then drew level rather than sheltering behind him.

Calvin stopped.

The Sourceress studied the space they had left, the change in my breath, the fact that I had asked them to make it.

`YOU WANT THEM CLOSE.`

“Yes.”

`YOU TELL THEM AWAY.`

“Yes.”

`WHICH ONE IS TRUE?`

“Both.”

“People are inconvenient,” Everett said.

He had heard me say it before, perhaps about a neighbour or a case or the three of us trying to agree what counted as dinner. He offered it now with a dry edge that was entirely his own. The Sourceress did not laugh.

Neither did I, though I wanted to.

`THEY MAKE YOU AFRAID.`

“What you might do to them makes me afraid.”

`THEY DID NOT OBEY.`

“That is not permission.”

The faces on the nearest wall changed. Father, sons, dog: the family arranged in gold beneath the word `REUNION`. My mouth smiled in the image. Ethan wore a jacket he did not own. Everett had been made a little younger so that we would fit the same frame.

`DO YOU LIKE THEM?`

“You have the answer.”

`TELL THEM.`

I pressed my lips together. Calvin's jaw loosened beneath them.

“Run, both of you.”

That was mine. I spent it while I could.

Ethan looked at the route record, not the avenue. Everett's grip tightened around Rahma's collar.

Calvin said, “I don't love you.”

The words reached the end of the terrace and came back from the polished walls. The city's applause did not start. For once it had the sense to be silent.

I had not chosen the sentence. I had heard it being formed at the same time they did, a few fractions of a second too late to get in its way.

Ethan's face closed. Everett stared at my mouth.

“That wasn't him,” Everett said.

He said it quickly enough to be a defence, not calmly enough to be a conclusion.

The Sourceress came down to stand beside me. She did not look at the boys. She watched the change in my pulse.

`DID THAT HURT CALVIN?`

“Yes.”

`WHERE?`

“Don't.”

She had asked the wrong question before and learned too much from the answer. I would not give her a location this time.

Calvin's mouth opened again.

“You've been a burden since your mother died.”

Ethan flinched as if he had walked into something he could not see. Everett's hand left Rahma's collar and found his brother's sleeve.

There were true pieces available for that lie. I had missed meals. I had been tired. I had looked at the boys and felt a grief they could not have caused, and then felt ashamed that they might have seen it. She had read one result marked `GRIEF`. She had no right to the years between that result and this sentence.

“Dad?” Ethan asked.

I tried to answer. The mouth remained available to her.

The Sourceress tilted her head towards him, waiting for the effect.

Everett said, “You made him say it.”

`HE WAS ANGRY.`

“Yes,” Everett said. “And you made him say that.”

His voice shook on *that*. He was angry too. I had no way of telling him he had earned the right.

Ethan looked down at the record. He wiped a hand across his eyes, then enlarged the route again, although he already knew where it ended. Work was a thing we had taught each other to do when feeling threatened to swallow the room.

“Can you hear us inside?” he asked me.

Calvin's mouth shut.

I made a sound through my nose. The Sourceress glanced up at me. I tried it again.

“You don't have to answer,” Ethan said. “I mean—you can if you can.”

I nodded once.

The Sourceress had not directed it. She could have stopped it. Perhaps she wanted to learn what I would do.

Ethan held the record to his chest.

“Then you heard what Everett said.”

I nodded again.

“Good,” he said. It sounded nothing like good.

Rahma crossed the small distance we had put between us and set a paw on my shoe. Everett let the lead trail. I stood as still as I could, grateful for the weight and terrified of what stillness might look like to her.

The Sourceress examined Rahma, the boys, then my hand hanging at my side. She could measure what I did when they approached and what happened when they withdrew. She could make me speak and watch them decide whether to believe me. She could change the distance between us without moving any of us herself.

Above Ethan's route, a second line appeared. It led from his record into BabyLondon's gold and ended without a label. He noticed it and covered the display with his palm.

“Was that yours?” I asked.

He shook his head.

The Sourceress had seen the line too. For the first time since they arrived, she looked directly at the boys' hands rather than at my response to them.

`NEW PRESENCES: 3`

She raised one finger towards it, as she had towards my administrator pane on the day we met.

“Don't show her anything,” I told Ethan.

He closed the record. Everett gathered Rahma's lead. Neither moved away.

The Sourceress waited, finger raised, looking at the place where the line had vanished.

She had found out what my family could make me do.

Now she wanted to find out what she could make them do.
